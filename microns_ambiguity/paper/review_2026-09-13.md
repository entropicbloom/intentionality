# Review: "What the correlations between neurons say about each neuron"

## 1. Summary

The paper asks whether the pairwise signal-correlation matrix of a sampled neural population, with the stimulus discarded and no neuron labelled at test time, determines each neuron's preferred orientation and receptive-field position. A transformer takes the 512x512 standardised correlation matrix of a sampled population, one row per neuron, and emits a per-neuron orientation vector or (x,y). On MICrONS (one mouse, 13 scans, 12,894 co-registered neurons) it reaches 25.4 degrees mean angular error in vivo and 20.2 on the digital twin against a stated 45-degree chance, and R^2 = 0.23 and 0.48 for receptive fields. The mechanism offered is that the class-averaged correlation matrix is nearly circulant, which fixes the orientation classes only up to the dihedral group D_8, and that a residual cardinal anisotropy (29 percent of class-Gram variance in vivo) supplies the absolute frame. Across 33 Allen mice, orientation transfers to held-out animals (about 36 degrees) only when sampled populations mix animals and only when relations are computed from drifting gratings, while single-animal populations sit within 3 degrees of chance, attributed to insufficient training diversity rather than to the circuit. Receptive fields transfer across animals only at the level of each animal's mean screen position, not the within-animal layout. The evidence is a set of decoding runs with 2 to 3 seeds and 3 splits, a symmetry decomposition with relabelling-matching, a synthetic von Mises population, and a battery of controls (scan centring, class balance, cross-stimulus Grams, permutation-equivariant variant, activity-based reference decoders).

## 2. Three strongest aspects

1. The protocol is unusually clean and unusually well explained. Figure 1d is the clearest description of a neuron-split decoding protocol I have seen: halves that share no neuron, sampled populations drawn inside a half, epoch chosen on a slice of the training half, and the explicit statement that the full matrix is used only in its training x training and test x test blocks. The four-cell Allen design in Figure 4 (split axis crossed with population axis) is exactly the right factorisation for the transfer question and should be borrowed by others.

2. The scan-identity control is genuinely convincing, and it is the control I most expected to fail. Centring predictions and labels per scan leaves receptive-field R^2 at 0.50 (twin) and 0.25 (in vivo), unchanged from absolute, and the variance decomposition ("Only 2% of the receptive-field label variance lies between scans and 9% between areas") caps what a scan-identity readout could possibly buy at 0.02. Combined with the rotation-per-scan check for orientation, this closes the earlier reviewer's point 3 properly.

3. The random-rotation training experiment in Section 3.2 is the single best idea in the paper. Training with each population's targets rotated at random, taking the loss after the best alignment, and then observing that the decoder still anchors a consistent frame on the data (raw error 25 and 31 degrees against 45 for an arbitrary frame) is a real answer to the "the reference lives in the weights" objection, because nothing in the loss ever told the decoder where zero is. This deserves more prominence than the third sentence of a four-observation paragraph.

## 3. Major concerns

**3.1. The synthetic experiment contradicts the conclusion drawn from it, and the text quotes only the rows that agree.** Section 3.2 states: "Adding an excess of cardinal frames to the stimulus, the strongest single anisotropy in this model, fixes the frame ($9^\circ$ to $15^\circ$ raw)". Table 5 has four rows containing the stimulus switch. Two work (stimulus: 15.1/15.1 and 9.2/9.2; sharper tuning + stimulus: 4.3/4.2 and 6.1/6.1). One fails in both seeds (count + stimulus: 52.6/35.8 and 43.8/27.4, no frame recovered at all). One fails in one seed and works in the other (all three: 45.9/44.0 against 12.1/12.1). The "all three" seed-0 cell is worse than the "none (circulant)" baseline on the aligned metric, 44.0 against 27.9, meaning that run recovered neither frame nor structure. A model in which the claimed frame-fixing manipulation is cancelled by adding a second, weaker anisotropy is not a model that demonstrates the claimed mechanism. The text never mentions these two rows. **Resolution:** run 5 to 10 seeds per cell, report mean and spread, and either explain the count + stimulus interaction (my guess: the count anisotropy dominates the sampled population composition and destabilises training) or withdraw the causal reading. Also state why the "sharper tuning (κ=8)" and "stronger cardinal gain" rows have only one seed.

**3.2. In cortex, the residual is never ablated, so Section 3.2 is correlational throughout.** The four observations all measure the residual or measure the decoder, never the decoder on a residual-free input. The one place a manipulation is possible is the synthetic model, where it fails as above. **Resolution:** feed the decoder a Gram from which the anisotropy has been removed, and show that the frame collapses while the aligned error survives. A concrete construction: for each neuron, regress its Gram row against a basis spanning the class-Gram residual estimated on the training half, subtract the fitted component, and re-run. If the raw error jumps toward 45 while the frame-corrected error stays near 20, the claim is demonstrated rather than asserted.

**3.3. A per-neuron scalar confound is not excluded, and the paper's own control shows it is large.** Appendix B reports: "With the three row statistics as the only input per neuron and no relational term, the error is $37.7^\circ$". That is 7 degrees below the stated chance level from three scalars per neuron with no relations at all. Those three statistics are never defined anywhere in the paper. If they include the row mean or row variance of the standardised correlation matrix, they are close to a measure of how strongly a neuron correlates with the population, which is confounded with responsiveness and with gOSI, and cardinal-preferring neurons in mouse V1 are on average more orientation-selective. The "cardinal anisotropy" could then be a density-of-selectivity effect read through a scalar rather than a property of the relational geometry. The class-balanced subsample control does not touch this, because it balances counts and not selectivity. **Resolution:** define the three statistics in Methods; report the correlation between each statistic and the distance to the nearest cardinal axis; re-run with cardinal and oblique neurons matched on gOSI and on row mean and variance; and report the standard decoder with the row statistics removed from the token.

**3.4. The chance baseline used in the abstract, every figure, and the "85% of the gain" calculation is the wrong one.** Methods assert: "A decoder that carries no information produces errors spread evenly over that range, whatever it outputs, so chance is $45^\circ$." That holds only for a uniform label marginal, and MICrONS labels are not uniform: "19\% of labelled neurons within $11^\circ$ of $0^\circ$, 25\% of $90^\circ$, 6 to 10\% in each oblique bin". The paper itself reports the correct null in Appendix B: "the best constant prediction has an error of $41.3^\circ$". The headline effect is therefore 41.3 to 25.4, not 45 to 25.4, and the abstract's "(an uninformed decoder: $45^\circ$)" is not accurate. The same substitution shifts the Appendix B claim that "the relations retain about 85\% of the gain over chance that the aligned activity gives", which is computed against 45. **Resolution:** use the label-prior baseline as the primary null everywhere, keep 45 only as the uniform-label reference, and recompute the retention fraction.

**3.5. The class-balanced numbers exist but are not the ones reported.** Appendix B gives "$24.5^\circ$ on the twin and $27.9^\circ$ in vivo" for the balanced metric, against 20.3 and 24.6 raw. The abstract and Table 1 quote the raw numbers. Given that the previous review round specifically requested class-balanced metrics, quoting the unbalanced number in the abstract and reporting the balanced one in an appendix paragraph is not a satisfying resolution. The coarse readouts have the same problem: "right for 79\% of neurons in vivo and 87\% on the twin, against 56\% for the majority answer" is a raw accuracy against a raw majority class, and no balanced version is given anywhere. **Resolution:** report the balanced error in Table 1 alongside the raw one, and give the balanced cardinal-axis accuracy.

**3.6. The Allen headline omits that it depends entirely on a grating stimulus.** The abstract says "Across 33 mice of the Allen Brain Observatory, orientation transfers to unseen animals only when a sampled population mixes animals". Section 3.3 reveals that with natural-movie relations "every test is at chance", and the result exists only with "the 40 blank-subtracted condition means of drifting gratings (8 directions $\times$ 5 temporal frequencies)". That is a designed, orientation-parameterised condition set, so the Allen correlation matrix is a tuning-profile similarity matrix over an orientation grid, which is much closer to classical RSA than to the movie-based MICrONS setup and much further from the paper's "discard the stimulus" premise. The decoder not seeing which condition is which preserves the frame question, but it does not preserve the "no stimulus" framing. **Resolution:** say "with grating relations" in the abstract, and state plainly in the discussion that the cross-animal result does not replicate on naturalistic relations.

**3.7. The single-animal-equals-chance dichotomy is asserted without a test, and the data suggest it is false.** All six single-animal values in Table 2 lie below 45 (42.5, 42.9, 42.1, 43.1, 43.1, 42.7), which under a true null of 45 would be a six-of-six sign pattern. The paper reads this as "Single-animal populations stay within $3^\circ$ of chance". The paper elsewhere argues these same populations do carry weak signal (MICrONS within-scan, 30.7 and 32.6 degrees). So the honest reading is a weak but consistent within-animal signal, not absence. The companion claim that mixed populations "are $7^\circ$ to $10^\circ$ below chance in both rows, at the same level, so transfer to a new brain has no detectable cost" is an equivalence claim from three splits with no equivalence test, and the two rows are 36.2 and 36.9 on average with split ranges that overlap heavily. **Resolution:** a permutation null (shuffle labels within the test set, refit nothing, recompute error) for the single-animal cells, and either a TOST-style equivalence statement or a downgrade of "no detectable cost" to "no cost detectable at this split count".

**3.8. The training-diversity explanation for the Allen single-animal failure is argued indirectly and the decisive control is missing.** The abstract says the single-animal failure is "a limit of the training diversity one animal provides rather than of its circuit". The two supporting controls are a pool-size sweep on mixed populations (280 or 560 neurons give 40.5 and 40.7 against 36.0 at 6,706) and the MICrONS within-scan result. Neither is the matched manipulation. The pool-size sweep shrinks the pool for mixed populations, which changes the number of distinct between-animal correlation patterns available, not the within-versus-between distinction. **Resolution:** restrict MICrONS training to a single scan with roughly 140 labelled training neurons, matching the Allen per-animal budget, and show that the MICrONS within-scan decoder also collapses to chance. That is a cheap run and it would convert the argument from indirect to direct. Relatedly, Table 4's row "populations drawn within one scan (128 neurons)" does not say whether the decoder was trained on within-scan or mixed-scan populations; if it was trained on mixed, the comparison is not matched.

**3.9. The in-vivo receptive-field result uses model-derived labels, and only the twin case is disclosed.** Methods state that MICrONS receptive-field centres come "from the twin (spike-triggered-average fit)". The abstract then reports "their receptive-field position at $\Rsq = 0.23$ ... from in-vivo responses in the MICrONS volume", and the discussion concedes circularity only for the twin: "the receptive-field labels themselves come from the twin, so the twin receptive-field result compares a model's correlations with the same model's receptive fields, while the orientation labels are in-vivo measurements throughout". The in-vivo receptive-field number is half model-derived too, and the sentence as written implies otherwise. **Resolution:** state it explicitly, and if any subset of MICrONS neurons has an in-vivo receptive-field estimate, report R^2 on that subset even if small.

**3.10. Uncertainty reporting is thin and inconsistent across tables.** Table 1 gives mean and s.d. over 3 seeds for the relational column but a bare point estimate for the "stimulus-known linear readout" column. Table 3 is "seed 0" only for every row, including the rows on which the central claims of Section 5 below rest. Table 4 is single numbers. Table 5 is two seeds with two cells having one. Table 2 lists three splits with no summary statistic. With effects of 3 to 9 degrees and split-to-split variance that the paper itself flags as exceeding seed variance (Fig. A4), several comparisons in the paper are within noise of each other as reported. **Resolution:** at minimum, 3 seeds and a spread for every row of Tables 3 and 4, and mean plus range in Table 2.

## 4. Does Section 3.2 demonstrate its claim?

It is much stronger than a bare assertion but it still stops short of demonstration, and the gap is specific.

What it establishes: the class-Gram is close to circulant (Fig. 3, 71 and 81 percent circulant variance); the residual is reproducible signal rather than noise or a count artefact (r = 0.94 across neuron halves, 0.84 and 0.99 across stimulus halves, 0.99 against class-balanced subsamples); label-prior predictors reach only 39 to 41 degrees while the decoder reaches 20 to 25; the decoder anchors a frame even when the loss never asks for one; and the errors have the shape of the anisotropy.

What it does not establish: that the residual is what supplies the frame. Every step is an association between two things measured on the same data. The weakest link is the second observation, which is also the one carrying the most interpretive weight: "the circulant part carries the whole per-neuron structure, and the residual adds the frame at no cost". The random-rotation decoder's input Gram still contains the residual. Removing the frame from the labels does not remove the residual from the input, so the experiment cannot separate the contribution of the circulant part from that of the residual. It shows that a frame can be anchored without label supervision, which is valuable, but not that the circulant part alone carries the structure.

The V1-to-AL transfer is the closest thing to a causal test and it is n = 1 in the relevant unit. The paper writes "trained on V1, scored on AL & twin & $58.2^\circ$ / $31.6^\circ$" and reads it as the anchor being the area's own anisotropy. AL differs from V1 in orientation selectivity, receptive-field size, spatial and temporal frequency preference, and neuron count, any of which could produce that pattern. Two things would make this convincing and neither is reported: the angle of the best rotation for AL, and whether that angle matches the phase difference between the V1 and AL residuals. If the AL rotation is, say, 42 degrees and the residual phase difference is also about 42 degrees, the mechanism is nailed. If they disagree, the reading fails. This is a one-line computation on results already in hand and it should be in the paper.

## 5. Is the reference-decoder framing sound?

Mostly yes, with one comparison that I consider unfair and one null that is over-read.

Sound: the architecture is the same transformer with a different token, which is the right control; the linear-readout row correctly shows that the activity transformer is essentially a per-neuron tuning readout with no population benefit, which is a useful and self-effacing finding; and the framing that what the activity decoder has over the relational one is stimulus alignment rather than extra information is correct, since the Gram is a deterministic function of the activity, making the activity decoder a proper upper bound.

Unfair: the cross-stimulus comparison in Table 3. "response vector, transformer, disjoint stimulus bins & $49.5^\circ$ & $41.0^\circ$", used in the text as "the activity decoder falls to $49.5^\circ$ in vivo and $41.0^\circ$ on the twin, at or below chance, while the relational decoder scores $34.2^\circ$ and $21.2^\circ$. The price buys stimulus independence". An activity decoder trained on bins 1 to 60 and tested on bins 61 to 120 has input coordinate k meaning a different movie frame at test than at train. Its failure is guaranteed by construction and carries no information about stimulus generalisation; it is a permuted-input test, not a transfer test. A relational input is invariant to this by definition, so the comparison is rigged. **Resolution:** either drop the row, or give the activity decoder a stimulus-invariant summary of its response vector (sorted response quantiles, a spectral summary, anything that survives a change of bin identity) so that the comparison tests something. As it stands it is the one place in the paper where an alternative method is set up to fail.

Over-read: the bin-permuted decoder. "response vector with bins shuffled per population, transformer & $27.1^\circ$ & $24.5^\circ$", concluded as "Pairwise relations are not missing anything a richer relational statistic would add here". This is a null result being used to support a positive claim, and the null has an obvious alternative explanation: the shuffled decoder must discover relational structure from scratch through attention under a fresh permutation each population, which is a far harder optimisation problem than being handed the Gram. Its failure bounds what this architecture and training budget can find, not what higher-order relations contain. The receptive-field collapse to R^2 = 0.04 and 0.08 makes this obvious, since the pairwise Gram reaches 0.23 and 0.48 on the identical information. **Resolution:** soften to "we found no evidence that a decoder free to compute richer statistics outperforms the Gram, though this may reflect the difficulty of learning them", or run a positive control by handing the decoder an explicit higher-order statistic, for example a Gram computed on squared or rectified responses.

Separately: Section 3.1 claims "the readout survives test Grams built from stimulus bins the decoder never trained on". In vivo the error goes from 25.4 to 34.2 degrees, a loss of two thirds of the gain over the label-prior baseline. "Survives" is too generous for the in-vivo case. The 60-bin control (25.3 degrees) does establish that the cost is stimulus sampling and not matrix size, and the twin's near-null cost at 2,500 bins supports the extrapolation, but the honest summary is that the in-vivo Gram over 120 movie bins is partly stimulus-specific and the paper should say so in Section 3.1 rather than only in the appendix.

Finally, Table 1's caption calls the linear readout "the ordinary tuning measurement". It is a linear map from a 120-bin movie response to (cos 2θ, sin 2θ), while the labels themselves came from gratings. It is a reasonable upper-bound reference but it is not the ordinary tuning measurement, and calling it that invites the reader to treat 20.2 degrees as a label-noise ceiling when it is not.

## 6. Does the synthetic experiment support the interpretation?

No, and I think it points the other way. Beyond the internal inconsistency in 3.1 above, there is a structural problem the paper does not confront.

In the model, the only manipulation that reliably fixes the frame is a stimulus anisotropy, an excess of cardinal frames in the stimulus ensemble. The two manipulations that are properties of the neurons rather than of the stimulus, an excess of cardinal-preferring neurons and sharper tuning at the cardinals, both fail on their own. The paper uses the first of these failures against the earlier reviewer: "The reviewer's alternative, that the frame could come from the uneven number of neurons per orientation, does not hold in the model". But it does not notice that the same table equally fails to support its own proposed mechanism, since the paper explicitly rules out a stimulus origin for the cortical residual in the very next clause: "the cortical residual is not stimulus-inherited either, since it reproduces across disjoint halves of the stimulus and differs between areas that saw the same movie". So the model demonstrates a mechanism that the paper says is not operating in cortex, and fails to demonstrate the mechanisms that could be. Reporting this honestly would mean saying that the synthetic exercise establishes that circulant relations genuinely cannot fix a frame (a useful sanity check for the D_8 argument) and that a sufficiently strong anisotropy of some kind can, but that it does not identify which cortical anisotropy does the work.

Two further methodological points on this table. First, the epoch is "selected on the frame-corrected error". Since the table's entire readout is the gap between raw and frame-corrected error, selecting on the frame-corrected metric systematically favours checkpoints with structure but no frame, which biases the table toward the "no frame" conclusion in exactly the rows the paper reads as negative results. Select on the raw error, or on both, and report the difference. Second, the synthetic runs use the 2.2M decoder while the MICrONS standard is 17M, and Figure A1 shows 0.3M is markedly worse, so capacity is not obviously irrelevant here.

A version of this experiment that would actually support the paper's reading: sweep the strength of a neuron-side anisotropy (density or sharpness) continuously with the stimulus held isotropic, and show that raw error drops below aligned error once the residual fraction of class-Gram variance reaches the 19 to 29 percent observed in cortex. That directly tests whether the observed cortical residual magnitude is sufficient.

## 7. Minor issues

1. The three row statistics are never defined. They appear in Methods ("together with three row statistics"), in the Appendix B control at 37.7 degrees, and in Table 4, without ever being named. This is a reproducibility hole, and per 3.3 above it may be load-bearing.
2. "Gram" is used throughout for a standardised, diagonal-zeroed correlation matrix. That is not a Gram matrix. Either define the term once as a convention or call it the correlation matrix.
3. Label counts do not reconcile. Methods: "33 mice, 9,281 cells". Figure A1 header: "5,529 in Allen". Section 3.3: "the full pool of 6,706". Three numbers, no explanation of the relationship.
4. MICrONS receptive-field R^2 appears as 0.234 and 0.480 in Table 1, as "0.50 absolute ... 0.25 ... in vivo" in the scan-control paragraph, and as 0.48 in the abstract. State which run each belongs to, or harmonise.
5. "the balanced error is $36.4^\circ$ against $35.7^\circ$ raw" for Allen. 35.7 matches none of Table 2's pooledcross values (35.2, 37.0, 38.4) nor their mean (36.9). Which run is this?
6. The augmentation claim is contradicted by its own numbers: "changes the Allen error by less than the seed spread in either direction ($35.2^\circ$, $33.5^\circ$, $37.7^\circ$ and $38.0^\circ$ ...)". That is a 4.5-degree range against a seed spread that Figure A4 shows as well under 1 degree. Appendix A repeats the claim. Either the spread is much larger than Figure A4 suggests, or the sentence is wrong.
7. The four-way readout is ill-defined for Allen. "the four-way split into $0^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $90^\circ$ and $135^\circ$ stays at its majority class (0.32 against 0.33)", but Allen labels are "six orientations $30^\circ$ apart", so 45 and 135 are never label values and the 30 and 60 degree labels are equidistant from two of the four classes. Drop this metric for Allen or define the tie-breaking rule.
8. Text and Figure A5 disagree for Allen. "neurons preferring $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$ are decoded to ... $25^\circ$ on Allen" but the Allen panel shows the 0-degree bin near 25 and the 90-degree bin near 34. The 120 and 150 degree bars sit exactly on the 45-degree chance line, which looks like clipping; if the bars are clipped, say so.
9. Figure A2's claim of saturation is not what the figure shows. Caption and Section 3.5 say "Population size saturates by 512 neurons in MICrONS and 256 on Allen", but the twin receptive-field curve rises from 0.28 to 0.31 between 512 and 1024, and the in-vivo orientation curve gets worse (27.7 to 29.4). Saturation and mild degradation are different findings.
10. Figure 1 has three problems: the title calls this a "label-free per-neuron decoding task" when training is fully supervised; panel c labels preferred orientation as "8 classes" while the paper decodes a continuous angle; and the panel-c footnote is truncated at the right margin ("11,326 with RF" runs off the page).
11. Table 4 mixes twin and in-vivo rows with no visual separation, and the orientation-balanced-loss row packs four numbers plus a parenthetical into a single cell. Split it into two columns (raw, balanced).
12. vaswani2017 is in refs.bib and cited nowhere.
13. Figure 6 reports within-mouse r as "-0.01, 0.11" while the text says "0.0 to 0.1". Trivial, but they should match.
14. Section 3.3 quotes "$22^\circ$ for mixed-scan populations of the same size" (128 neurons), which appears in no table. Add it to Table 4 so the within-scan comparison is checkable.
15. Four TODO placeholders remain: the author footnote ("TODO: affiliation and co-authors"), "Code and data. TODO: repository URL", Appendix E ("TODO: ridge readouts ...") and Appendix F ("TODO: per-split table ..."). Appendix E in particular contains numbers the reader needs, including the acknowledgement that "the decoder exceeded it on Allen receptive fields".
16. Two figures are referenced as evidence for the mechanism but Figure 3's right panel is hard to read as presented: the circulant-projection bars for both substrates sit at roughly 0.10 against a chance line at about 0.125, so the projected matrices are at or slightly below chance. Saying "absolute accuracy collapses to chance" is fine, but a reader tracking the bars will see them below the line and wonder.

## 8. Verdict

Venue fit: the question, the protocol, and the symmetry framing are a good match for PLoS Computational Biology, and the paper would be a strong NeurIPS workshop submission essentially as is. For a journal I would return it as major revision. The empirical core (Sections 3.1, 3.3, 3.4 and the controls appendix) is solid and the earlier reviewer's points 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 are substantially addressed. Point 1 is addressed in substance by the random-rotation and cross-stimulus experiments but the abstract still oversells it. Point 2 is not addressed, because the controls requested for it produce a result that undercuts the paper's mechanism and the text reports only the agreeing half.

Before submission:

- Fix the null. Use the label-prior baseline (41.3 degrees) as the primary chance level in the abstract, Table 1, all figures, and the "85 percent of the gain" calculation, and promote the class-balanced errors (24.5 and 27.9) into Table 1.
- Either ablate the anisotropy from the input Gram in the real data and show the frame collapses, or rewrite Section 3.2 as a set of consistent observations rather than as an identified mechanism. Report the best-rotation angle for V1-to-AL and check it against the residual phase difference.
- Rerun the synthetic table with 5 or more seeds, epoch-selected on the raw error, and confront the count + stimulus and all-three rows in the text. As it stands the table does not support the sentence it is cited for.
- Remove or repair the disjoint-stimulus-bin row for the activity decoder, which cannot succeed by construction, and soften the bin-permuted null from "pairwise relations are not missing anything" to what an underpowered null supports.
- Define the three row statistics, characterise how much of the 37.7-degree row-statistics-only result is a selectivity confound with distance-to-cardinal, add the matched MICrONS 140-training-neuron single-scan run that would make the Allen diversity claim direct, note the grating dependence of the Allen result in the abstract, disclose that the in-vivo receptive-field labels are twin-derived, and fill the four TODOs.
