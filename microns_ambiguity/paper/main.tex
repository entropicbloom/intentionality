\begin{abstract}
What a neuron represents is usually established by relating its activity to a stimulus. We ask whether the same facts are contained in the correlation matrix of a recorded population, which keeps the pairwise relations among the neurons and discards the stimulus. A transformer that sees only the correlation matrix of 512 sampled neurons, with neither labels nor any labelled neuron in its input, decodes the preferred orientation of mouse visual cortex neurons from in-vivo MICrONS responses to a mean error of $25^\circ$ (uninformed: $45^\circ$; best constant guess: $41^\circ$; $28^\circ$ when the error is averaged over eight orientation bins rather than over neurons) and their receptive-field position, as fitted by the digital twin, at $\Rsq = 0.23$; on the digital-twin substrate, $20^\circ$ ($24.5^\circ$ balanced) and 0.48. Asked whether a neuron prefers horizontal or vertical, it is right for 79\% of neurons in vivo and 87\% on the twin, against 56\% for the majority answer; it does not resolve the obliques. The ordering is recoverable because the relations between orientation classes are close to circulant, which fixes the class structure up to rotation and reflection; the rotation of the frame is recoverable only because of a departure from that symmetry. The departure survives equal representation of every orientation and disjoint training and test stimuli, and the candidate departure is an anisotropy of the signal correlations: the neurons preferring one orientation, $90^\circ$ in V1, have the most similar responses to one another, and a model population with that anisotropy and nothing else, with equal numbers of neurons per orientation, recovers the frame; the anisotropy could not be removed from the cortical data. Across 33 mice of the Allen Brain Observatory, with relations from drifting gratings, orientation transfers to unseen animals only when a sampled population mixes animals; populations drawn from one animal are not decoded measurably below the best constant. An Allen animal supplies only 128 to 333 training cells, so its populations are nearly one population, whereas single-scan populations in MICrONS, drawn from scans of about 1,000 neurons, reach $31^\circ$ to $32^\circ$, $9^\circ$ to $10^\circ$ below the MICrONS best constant of $41.3^\circ$. For receptive fields, what crosses animals is each animal's mean receptive-field position, and only weakly the position of a neuron relative to its mouse-mates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A neuron in visual cortex can be characterised by what it responds to: vertical gratings, or the upper left of the screen. This is conventionally established with the stimulus. We ask whether the same facts are contained in a description that mentions no stimulus. For a population that watched the same movie, one can compute the correlation of every pair of neurons and then discard the movie. Does the resulting matrix determine which neuron prefers which orientation, and where each neuron's receptive field lies?

The idea that content can be read from relations is old. Shepard and Chipman proposed that a representation need not resemble what it stands for as long as the similarities among representations mirror the similarities among the things represented \citep{shepard1970}, and representational similarity analysis turned this into a method that compares brains and models through their similarity structures rather than their coordinates \citep{kriegeskorte2008}. Structuralist accounts of experience claim that what a state represents, or how it feels, is fixed by its position in a space of relations \citep{lyre2022}. \citet{framework2025} formulates the corresponding question for neural networks and gives the decoding framework we use here.

Two lines of work come close. \citet{tsodyks1999} showed that the spike-triggered average of spontaneous population activity reproduces the map of a neuron's preferred orientation, with no stimulus present; the tuning was read from that pattern by comparison with the evoked map, a stimulus-labelled reference. Unsupervised manifold methods recover a neuron's tuning from the structure of population activity alone, without measuring the variable it encodes \citep{rubin2019}, but the recovered coordinate is defined only up to the symmetries of the latent space, and it is related to the measured variable by aligning to it afterwards. In both cases the relations locate a neuron within the structure of the encoded variable, and something outside the relations, the evoked map or the measured behaviour, says which value that location corresponds to. We ask whether the relations among neurons fix the content of each neuron when the stimulus is discarded and the population being read contains no labelled neuron, no map and no behaviour to compare with: our decoder is trained on labelled neurons, but they are other neurons, and on Allen other animals. We also ask whether that content is readable across thousands of individually identified neurons and across animals. The nearest machine-learning work reads properties of a neuron from population activity: NuCLR learns a neuron's identity from its population context with a permutation-equivariant transformer and transfers cell type and brain region to unseen animals \citep{arora2025}. It sees the activity itself. Our decoder sees only the correlation matrix, so it answers what the relations alone determine, and its target is stimulus tuning rather than cell class. This relational setting is common: recordings under natural behaviour have no controlled stimulus, and neurons from different sessions or animals share no stimulus-aligned coordinates, but they do share the structure of their correlations.

We answer this question with a decoder whose input is the correlation matrix of a sampled population, one row per neuron, and nothing else, and whose output is, per neuron, either a preferred orientation or a receptive-field position. It is trained on labelled populations and scored on neurons it never saw, at two scales. Within one brain we use the MICrONS release \citep{ding2025,microns2025}: 12,894 neurons from a cubic millimetre of mouse visual cortex, with in-vivo responses to a shared movie, a fitted digital-twin model \citep{wang2025}, orientation labels for 5,287 of them and receptive-field labels for 11,326 (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). The decoder trained on half the neurons reads the other half. Across brains we use the Allen Brain Observatory \citep{devries2020}: 33 mice recorded under identical stimuli. The decoder trained on some animals reads neurons of animals it never saw, which succeeds only when a sampled population mixes animals, and only with grating relations.

Averaging the correlations by orientation class shows that the relations between classes are almost circulant, which fixes the classes only up to a rotation or reflection of the orientation circle. A consistent departure from that symmetry fixes the absolute frame and makes the readout possible.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

\paragraph{MICrONS.} The node table released with \citet{ding2025} holds 12,894 neurons co-registered to the electron-microscopy volume, across V1, RL, AL and LM, from 13 of the 14 two-photon scans: the authors' working set after their quality restrictions, applied to the 13,952 matched neurons they report. Each neuron has a trial-averaged in-vivo response to the oracle natural-movie clips (120 bins), shown in every scan; a digital-twin response to a shared movie (4,999 bins, which we compress to 512 principal components; the Gram is preserved to $r > 0.999$); an in-vivo preferred orientation with a global orientation selectivity index (gOSI); and a receptive-field centre from the twin (spike-triggered-average fit, screen coordinates in $[-1,1]$). The orientation label is the preferred orientation of the in-vivo tuning curve to Monet, a directional noise movie shown in 16 directions $22.5^\circ$ apart in a separate block of the same scans, and gOSI is computed from that curve \citep{ding2025}, so on MICrONS too the labels and the relations come from different stimuli. We use orientation, as a continuous angle, for the 5,287 neurons with gOSI $\geq 0.25$, and receptive-field position for the 11,326 neurons whose twin test correlation is $\geq 0.2$. The orientation labels are in-vivo measurements on both substrates (the release also carries a twin orientation, fitted the same way to the twin's Monet responses, which we use only to bound label noise in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and never as a target); the receptive-field labels come from the twin's fit on both substrates, so every receptive-field result, in vivo included, scores relations against model-derived positions (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Same-scan pairs correlate more strongly than cross-scan pairs at matched receptive-field distance: with the pairs of 6,000 receptive-field-labelled neurons binned by decile of centre distance, the same-scan mean is 1.4 to 1.6 times the cross-scan mean in vivo and 1.2 to 1.4 times on the twin over the six nearest deciles. The dependence of the correlation on receptive-field distance, and on the difference in preferred orientation (in vivo, 0.074 within and 0.061 across scans for pairs less than $15^\circ$ apart), has the same sign and shape within and across scans; only the level differs. All populations mix scans, and the scan and area centring controls bound what scan identity contributes (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). Only 2\% of the receptive-field label variance lies between scans and 9\% between areas, so a decoder that read scan or area identity from the Gram could reach at most that much $\Rsq$.

\paragraph{Allen.} From Visual Coding 2P \citep{devries2020} we take the 36 VISp excitatory containers with at least 150 cells significantly responsive to drifting gratings: 33 mice (three contribute two containers each; splits are taken over mice, so all containers of a mouse fall on the same side of every held-out-animal split), 9,281 cells, of which 5,529 carry the static-grating orientation label used here; unlabelled cells stay in the populations and receive no loss, so a held-out-animal split has 6,706 training cells and 2,575 test cells in total. Relations come from session A, either responses to natural movies shown to every mouse (4,500 bins) or the 40 blank-subtracted condition means of drifting gratings (8 directions $\times$ 5 temporal frequencies). Orientation labels come from static gratings in session B (six orientations $30^\circ$ apart, gOSI $\geq 0.25$, $p < 0.05$), so labels and relations use different stimuli. Receptive-field centres come from locally sparse noise in session C (1,504 cells with a significant fit, 23 to 112 per mouse).

\subsection{Relations}

Each neuron's response vector is centred and normalised; the relation between two neurons is the cosine of their vectors, a signal correlation. For a population of $n$ neurons the Gram is the $n\times n$ matrix of these values, standardised by an off-diagonal mean and standard deviation estimated once per run from the correlation matrix of 2,000 neurons drawn at random from the dataset, with the diagonal zeroed. These are two scalars, not per-neuron information; estimating them on the training neurons alone, or on each sampled population, moves them by at most 0.001 on either substrate. We call this standardised correlation matrix the Gram throughout.

\subsection{Decoder}

Each row of a population's Gram, together with three row statistics (the mean, standard deviation and third moment of the row), is projected to a token; $L$ pre-norm transformer blocks \citep{vaswani2017,xiong2020} with $H$-head attention update the tokens, the Gram entering the attention logits as an additive bias; a linear head reads out, per token, the unit vector $(\cos 2\theta, \sin 2\theta)$ of the preferred orientation $\theta$ or an $(x,y)$ receptive-field position. The loss is the mean squared error on the labelled tokens only; unlabelled neurons stay in the population and receive no loss. The standard configuration is width 512, 8 layers and 17M parameters with populations of 512 neurons on MICrONS, and width 256, 4 layers and 2.2M parameters with populations of 256 neurons on Allen. Other sizes are in Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}. Training uses AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), a one-cycle schedule with peak learning rate $10^{-3}$ and 10\% warm-up, 30 epochs of 5,000 populations in batches of 64, 4 attention heads and dropout 0.1 on MICrONS; on Allen, 12 epochs of 2,500 populations of 128 neurons in batches of 16. Early stopping keeps the epoch with the lowest error on a selection slice: a random 20\% of the labelled training half on MICrONS (528 of 2,643 neurons for orientation), whole mice held out of the training set on Allen. The same configuration is used for every run; only width, depth and population size vary (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}).

\subsection{Protocol}

\paragraph{Split.} MICrONS neurons are halved at random. Training populations are sampled inside the training half and test populations inside the test half, so no test neuron appears in a training population (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}d). Early stopping uses a slice of the training half: the epoch with the lowest error on that slice is kept, so the test half never influences model selection.

\paragraph{Regimes (Allen).} Two independent choices define a run. The split decides where test neurons come from: the training animals, each halved into training and test neurons, or held-out animals. The population decides what a sample is: neurons drawn from one animal, so that the Gram is a within-circuit matrix, or from several animals, so that most entries are between-animal correlations. We call the four cells \code{within}, \code{pooledwithin}, \code{cross} and \code{pooledcross}. In every regime training populations are drawn from training neurons and test populations from test neurons, so no test population contains a neuron the decoder was trained on; in the training-animal regimes the test neurons come from animals some of whose other neurons were in training. A held-out-animal split holds out 9 of the 33 mice. Held-out regimes select the epoch on further held-out mice. Every held-out cell is run on three mouse splits and every training-animal cell on three neuron splits.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Orientation is scored by the mean absolute angular error between the decoded angle, $\tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}$ of the output, and the label, taken modulo $180^\circ$, so it lies between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. A decoder whose output is independent of the label produces errors spread evenly over that range when either the labels or the outputs are uniform on the orientation circle, so chance is $45^\circ$. With the MICrONS label distribution the best constant prediction scores $41.3^\circ$ (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). Allen labels sit on a $30^\circ$ grid, so a decoder that recovered every true preference exactly would still disagree with them by $7.5^\circ$ on average. A frame check reports the error after the best single rotation or reflection of all decoded angles. If it is lower than the raw error, the decoder recovered the structure but not the frame. Receptive fields are scored by $\Rsq$ against a baseline of 0.

\subsection{Symmetry analysis}

To expose the symmetry of the relations we average the Gram into a $K\times K$ class-Gram, the mean relation between neurons of two orientation classes, project it onto its circulant part, and ask which relabellings a held-out half's class-Gram is compatible with (all $K!$ relabellings, Frobenius distance). A relabelling that differs from the truth by a rotation or a reflection of the orientation circle is an element of the dihedral group $D_K$. The analysis reports whether the truth is singled out absolutely or only modulo $D_K$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Per-neuron content from the correlation matrix alone}\label{sec:main}

On MICrONS the decoder reads orientation and receptive-field position from the Gram of 512 neurons of the test half (Fig.~\ref{fig:decoder}, Table~\ref{tab:microns}). The twin substrate is cleaner, since the twin removes trial noise. The predictions are in the true frame: the frame check lowers the error by less than $0.1^\circ$ in every run. About half the neurons are decoded to within $15^\circ$ (0.46 in vivo, 0.54 on the twin), but the error is not spread evenly over the orientation circle: the decoder resolves the cardinal axes and little else (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}, Fig.~\ref{fig:bins}). Asked only which cardinal axis a neuron is closer to, it is right for 87\% of all neurons on the twin and 79\% in vivo (majority answer 56\%; averaged over the eight true-orientation bins, 83\% and 74\%), and for the neurons whose preference lies within $15^\circ$ of an axis, for 94\% and 86\% (neuron splits 1 and 2). Those near-axis neurons are 1,445 and 1,482 of the 2,644 test neurons of the two splits. Sorted into four classes at $0^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $90^\circ$ and $135^\circ$, 66\% (twin) and 59\% (in vivo) land in the right one against a majority class of 40\%. Within $15^\circ$ of a cardinal axis the error is $12^\circ$ on the twin and $19^\circ$ in vivo; within $15^\circ$ of an oblique, $36^\circ$ to $39^\circ$ over both substrates, near chance (Fig.~\ref{fig:bins}). Both readouts are properties of individual neurons, not of the scan: centring predictions and labels per scan leaves the receptive-field $\Rsq$ unchanged and the orientation error within $0.2^\circ$ (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). Test Grams from stimulus bins the decoder never trained on add about $0.5^\circ$ of error on the twin and $8.5^\circ$ in vivo, where a Gram over 60 movie bins is partly specific to those bins (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}).

\subsection{Why the absolute frame is recoverable}\label{sec:frame}

The class-Gram of orientation is close to circulant (Fig.~\ref{fig:symmetry}): neighbouring orientations correlate, orthogonal ones anti-correlate, and the pattern repeats around the circle. A circulant matrix is unchanged by every rotation and reflection of the orientation circle, so that part of the relations fixes the classes only up to the 16 elements of $D_8$ and cannot say which class is $0^\circ$. Whatever fixes the frame must be a departure from circulant structure, at the class level or finer. At the class level that residual holds 29\% of the class-Gram variance in vivo and 19\% on the twin over the whole volume (30\% and 16\% in V1), and it is signal: it correlates at 0.94 between random halves of the neurons on both substrates, at 0.84 (in vivo) and 0.99 (twin) between disjoint halves of the stimulus bins, and at 0.99 with the residual computed on class-balanced subsamples, so it is neither a finite-stimulus artefact nor a consequence of the uneven number of neurons per orientation. Its form is one-peaked, not bi-cardinal: in vivo, neurons preferring orientations within about $45^\circ$ of $90^\circ$ correlate more with each other than the circulant baseline predicts and the rest less, and on both substrates the same-class excess at $90^\circ$ exceeds that at $0^\circ$ (V1: $+0.036$ against $-0.011$ in vivo, $+0.014$ against $+0.006$ on the twin, where the gap between the two is smaller), and its shape differs between areas (residual correlation V1--RL 0.77, V1--AL 0.31).

Four observations narrow down what fixes the frame.

\emph{Priors do not explain the accuracy.} The decoder reaches $20^\circ$ to $25^\circ$, while predictors that use only the distribution of labels, the best constant orientation or each scan's or area's mean, reach $39^\circ$ to $41^\circ$. Whether the frame comes from the label density is settled by the class-balanced populations below.

\emph{The structure is recoverable with no frame in the labels.} A decoder trained with each population's targets rotated at random, and the loss taken after the best alignment of predictions to targets, places neurons to $19.9^\circ$ on the twin and $25.5^\circ$ in vivo after one global alignment, the same as the standard decoder with the frame ($20.2^\circ$, $25.4^\circ$). This does not show that the circulant part alone carries the structure, since the residual is still in that decoder's input. Its raw error, $25^\circ$ and $31^\circ$ against $45^\circ$ for an arbitrary frame, shows that it fixes a consistent frame from the data even when the loss does not ask for one.

\emph{The frame is not the label density and not the particular sample of frames.} With every training and test population drawn with equal numbers of neurons from each of the eight orientation classes, the decoder reaches $22.0^\circ$ on the twin and $26.6^\circ$ in vivo (two seeds each), with no gap between raw and aligned error. The frame also survives training and test relations built from disjoint halves of the stimulus, though both halves share the movie's orientation content; on Allen, where the grating conditions cover orientation uniformly and the labels are nearly flat, the readout is cardinal all the same (Section~\ref{sec:allen}), and it survives removing the three row statistics from the input ($20.3^\circ$), while the statistics alone fix only a weak frame ($37.7^\circ$; Appendix~\ref{app:controls}).

\emph{The errors are smallest at the cardinal axes and largest at the obliques.} They are $11^\circ$ to $12^\circ$ near the cardinal axes and $37^\circ$ to $39^\circ$ in the two oblique classes on the twin, and reweighting the loss so that obliques count as much as cardinals leaves them unchanged ($11^\circ$ and $14^\circ$ at the cardinals, $34^\circ$ and $41^\circ$ at the obliques; Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). The oblique ordering is not in the relations at single-neuron resolution.

\emph{In V1 the neurons of the dominant orientation correlate most strongly with each other.} Define the anisotropy factor as the mean correlation between two neurons of the same orientation class, at the class where it peaks, divided by its mean over the other seven classes. In V1, where 28\% of neurons fall in the $22.5^\circ$ class centred on $90^\circ$, the factor peaks at $90^\circ$ on both substrates (1.28 on the twin, 1.77 in vivo; Table~\ref{tab:areas}), and $90^\circ$ is the peak in 99\% of neuron bootstraps on both substrates, and the values are unchanged on class-balanced subsamples, so the signature is in the correlations, not in the counts. The coincidence therefore holds in V1, which supplies 73\% of the labelled neurons, and the whole volume peaks at $90^\circ$ too (Table~\ref{tab:areas}); of the two smaller areas it holds in AL on the twin and in neither substrate in RL, three of the six area$\times$substrate cells in all. The quantity is a signal correlation over trial-averaged responses, and on the twin over noiseless ones, so the anisotropy is one of tuning similarity, not of trial-to-trial co-fluctuation. Cardinal over-representation and its variation between areas are established features of mouse visual cortex \citep{roth2012,fahey2019}, and the preferential connectivity of like-tuned neurons \citep{ko2011} is one route by which their responses could become more similar.

\emph{A model population tests the candidate.} We could not isolate what fixes the frame by subtraction, because removing the class-level residual from every Gram with the true classes injects the classes into the Gram (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}), so the frame is located by exclusion and by a model (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}, Table~\ref{tab:syn}). Model neurons with von Mises tuning, a movie-like stimulus that covers orientation evenly, and the same pipeline give a class-Gram circulant to within noise, and the decoder trained on it recovers the ordering, $30^\circ$ after one global alignment, but no frame, $54^\circ$ raw. Sharper cardinal tuning and a cardinal excess of neurons fix the frame in at most one seed of five, alone, together, or at any strength tested; an excess of cardinal orientations among the stimulus frames does in three of five. Adding only the anisotropy the data show, a shared stimulus-driven response component among like-tuned neurons peaked at one orientation, with uniform counts and an even stimulus, fixes the frame in every seed at every strength run (15 of 15, $2^\circ$ to $4^\circ$ raw), from a residual weaker than the twin V1's (0.05 of class-Gram variance against 0.16) to one between the twin's and the in-vivo V1 value (0.23 against 0.30); no strength tested fails, so no threshold was located; the weakest setting is below cortex on the residual fraction and above it on the anisotropy factor (1.67 against 1.28 on the twin), so the model is neither uniformly weaker nor uniformly stronger than cortex. The same field with a uniform weight, so that no orientation is preferred, does not fix the frame (one seed, $41.6^\circ$ raw against $40.4^\circ$ aligned): its residual falls from 0.15 to 0.015 of the class-Gram variance and its peak class varies with the seed, so what fixes the frame is the peak of the weight, not the shared component as such. With training and test relations from disjoint halves of the frames, as on cortex, the peaked field still fixes the frame (two seeds, $7.0^\circ$ and $16.6^\circ$ raw) and the uniform one does not ($49.0^\circ$ raw, $40.0^\circ$ aligned). The model residual has the one-peaked form of the cortical one and correlates 0.39 to 0.48 with it in vivo, against 0.94 for the cortical residual with itself across neuron halves. The model is far easier to decode than cortex ($2^\circ$ against $20^\circ$ at a similar residual), so it shows that the anisotropy suffices, not that it accounts for the cortical error.

\subsection{Orientation transfers between animals through mixed populations}\label{sec:allen}

With natural-movie relations, orientation is nearly absent from Allen correlations: same-orientation pairs correlate 0.005 more than orthogonal pairs, against 0.09 in MICrONS, and the class-level tests are at or just above chance (Fig.~\ref{fig:a4}). The Allen results below therefore use grating relations, a designed set of 40 conditions over an orientation grid, and do not replicate on naturalistic relations. With gratings the signal is present (0.05 within a mouse, 0.044 across mice) and the four regimes separate (Fig.~\ref{fig:allen2x2}, Table~\ref{tab:allen}). Single-animal populations are close to uninformative: all six runs lie $1.9^\circ$ to $2.9^\circ$ below the $45^\circ$ uninformed level, but only $0.3^\circ$ to $1.0^\circ$ below the best constant of their own test set ($42.5^\circ$ to $43.9^\circ$ per split), and one of the six is $0.4^\circ$ above it. A margin of that size is inside the sampling noise of a test set of about 1,500 cells (bootstrap s.e.\ $0.9^\circ$ over cells, $1.4^\circ$ clustered on the nine test mice), so the six runs show a consistent direction, not a resolved effect, whether or not the animal was seen in training, and on every split the mixed populations of the same regime are $4.3^\circ$ to $7.9^\circ$ better (mixed populations of 128 neurons, matching the single-animal size, score $36.0^\circ$; Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). Mixed populations are $6.6^\circ$ to $9.8^\circ$ below chance in both rows: $36.2^\circ$ on the training animals and $36.9^\circ$ on held-out ones. The two rows use different splits, of neurons and of animals, so they cannot be differenced split by split; with a split-to-split spread of about $1.5^\circ$ in each row, three splits bound the cost of transfer to a new brain at about $3^\circ$, not at zero. As on MICrONS the readout is cardinal: over the three splits the decoder names the right cardinal axis for 62\% of all cells and 68\% of the cells labelled $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$ (majority 51\% and 52\%; 61\% averaged over the six label bins), cells labelled $0^\circ$ are decoded to $28^\circ$ and those labelled $90^\circ$ to $36^\circ$, cells labelled $120^\circ$ or $150^\circ$ are decoded at or above the $45^\circ$ chance level ($49^\circ$ and $51^\circ$ pooled, $45^\circ$ to $56^\circ$ over the three splits), their predictions being pulled onto the cardinal axes, and the four-class readout is at the majority class (Fig.~\ref{fig:bins}). What matters is whether the Gram contains between-animal correlations. These exist only because all mice saw the same 40 conditions, and they measure how similar two tuning profiles are. The decoder still never sees which condition is which, so the absolute frame must again come from the population's relational structure. Orientation is thus readable from a correlation geometry that spans animals, and only weakly from relations inside one circuit. Two controls locate the limit. The limit is not the neurons: with each neuron's own 40-condition response vector as its token, the same decoder on the same single-animal populations reaches $33^\circ$ to $35^\circ$, as with mixed populations (Table~\ref{tab:allen}, last row). The limit is not the size of the training set either: the single-animal runs are trained on 3,451 to 6,927 cells, the same pool as the mixed-population runs, and only the sampling of each population is confined to one animal. What they lack is diversity. An animal has about 280 cells, about 170 of them labelled, so at 128 neurons each training Gram is drawn from a pool of 128 to 333 cells, and one animal's populations are nearly one population. Shrinking the pool instead, with mixed populations of 128 drawn from 280 or 560 training cells, costs $4.5^\circ$ ($40.5^\circ$ and $40.7^\circ$ against $36.0^\circ$ from the full pool of 6,706; single runs, Appendix~\ref{app:controls}), less than the $6^\circ$ to $8^\circ$ that confining populations to one animal costs. MICrONS separates the two: a decoder trained on 140 neurons of one scan and tested on the other scans scores $38^\circ$ to $44^\circ$ on the twin and $37^\circ$ to $39^\circ$ in vivo (three scans), while with the full training pool of one animal, single-scan populations of 128 are still decoded only to $32.0^\circ$ on the twin and $31.1^\circ$ in vivo, against $22.3^\circ$ and $25.8^\circ$ for mixed-scan populations of the same size (three seeds each; Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). A cost of reading inside one circuit remains where the pool is no longer the constraint.

\subsection{Receptive fields: what crosses animals is the animal's screen position}\label{sec:rf}

Correlation falls with receptive-field distance within a mouse (0.127 at $<5^\circ$ to 0.068 beyond $40^\circ$) and across mice (0.049 to 0.011). Decoding absolute screen coordinates on held-out mice gives $\Rsq$ that varies with the split, from $0.29$ to $-0.45$ for single-animal populations and from $0.22$ to $-0.25$ for mixed ones. Decomposing the saved predictions explains the variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:rf}, Appendix~\ref{app:rfsplits}). The correlation between predicted and true mean position of each held-out mouse, pooled over 27 mouse-level predictions, is 0.36 to 0.71 (permutation $p \leq 0.04$). The correlation between predicted and true position of a neuron relative to its mouse-mates is $-0.01$ to 0.11 over 1,259 cells. The decoder recovers the mean receptive-field position of an animal's imaged patch, and the position of a neuron within that patch only weakly: the largest of the four within-mouse correlations, the vertical coordinate under mixed populations, is 0.11 with a permutation $p$ of 0.001 against shuffles inside each mouse; the other three are 0.07, 0.06 and $-0.01$ ($p$ = 0.02, 0.07 and 0.6). $\Rsq$ on absolute coordinates then depends mostly on whether the between-mouse spread of a particular test set is reproduced at the right scale.

The within-mouse layout is present in the correlations, since same-mouse correlation falls with distance. It is not learned because each mouse contributes only 23 to 112 labelled cells. With 11,326 labels in one animal, MICrONS lets the same architecture reach neuron-level receptive fields at $\Rsq = 0.48$ with the 17M decoder, and 0.44 at the Allen decoder's size of 2.2M (the 2.2M point of Fig.~\ref{fig:a1} reaches only 0.34 on the shorter schedule used for that curve).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

A decoder trained on a disjoint set of labelled neurons, and given at test time nothing but the correlation matrix of a few hundred cortical neurons, predicts each neuron's preferred orientation to a mean error of $25^\circ$ in vivo and $20^\circ$ on the digital twin, against $45^\circ$ for an uninformed decoder and $41^\circ$ for the best constant guess. The readout is cardinal: near-cardinal neurons to $12^\circ$ to $19^\circ$, obliques near chance. It predicts each neuron's receptive-field position to $\Rsq = 0.23$ in vivo and 0.48 on the digital twin. The symmetry analysis constrains the mechanism: the relations fix the class structure up to the symmetries of the content, and a departure from that symmetry fixes the frame. That departure is a property of the correlations themselves: it is not the uneven number of neurons per orientation and not the particular stimulus sample. In V1 the neurons preferring the over-represented orientation have the most similar responses to one another, whether or not the sampled population is imbalanced. A direct ablation of that feature in the data is not available, but a model population with that anisotropy and nothing else recovers the absolute frame at every strength tested, while models with sharper or more numerous cardinal neurons do not; a cardinal excess among the stimulus frames also fixes it, in three seeds of five, and the orientation content of the movie was not measured.

Across animals the two contents behave differently. Orientation is carried by the relations between tuning profiles, which exist between any two neurons that saw the same conditions, in the same brain or not. From the correlations inside a single scan it is readable only weakly, at $31^\circ$ to $32^\circ$ on MICrONS, and inside a single Allen animal barely, where 140 training cells are too few to learn from. Receptive-field position is carried at the level of an animal's patch, and the neuron-level layout needs more labels per animal than these recordings provide.

Limits. MICrONS is one animal, and its training and test halves share recording sessions. The Allen held-out-animal cells are the cleaner generalisation test, but their variance across mouse splits is large, and they exist only with grating relations, a designed 40-condition set on which two tuning profiles can be compared, not with the naturalistic relations of the MICrONS result. The readout is cardinal: the obliques are not resolved on either dataset, so the mean error understates the precision at the cardinals and overstates it elsewhere. No experiment removes the residual from the decoder's input, so the assignment of the ordering to the circulant part and the frame to the residual is inferred from the symmetry argument and the model, not measured. The mechanism is established by exclusion and by a model, not by removing the candidate anisotropy from cortical data, since the direct ablation we tried injected the labels it was meant to remove (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). Orientation labels are noisy in both datasets (in vivo and twin labels agree on 70\% of MICrONS neurons at 8 classes; static and drifting gratings on 45 to 67\% of Allen cells), and the Allen labels are quantised to $30^\circ$, which puts a $7.5^\circ$ floor under every Allen error, relational and stimulus-known alike. The twin substrate is a model fitted to the same recordings, so the in-vivo results carry the claim about cortex and the twin results show what cleaner correlations would give; in AL the two substrates place the anisotropy peak two classes apart ($0^\circ$ on the twin, $135^\circ$ in vivo), though the in-vivo peak is within noise of three other classes at 294 neurons. The receptive-field labels come from the twin for both substrates, so the twin receptive-field result compares a model's correlations with the same model's receptive fields, and the in-vivo result scores cortical correlations against model-estimated positions. The orientation labels are in-vivo measurements throughout.

The symmetry result is an instance of a general expectation: a representation that is equivariant to a group has a group-fixed part, identical across individuals up to relabelling, and an individual remainder \citep{oizumi2026}. The matching-over-relabellings test is the discrete form of unsupervised alignment of similarity structures across individuals by optimal transport \citep{kawakita2025}. This paper adds the measurement in cortex, at the level of single neurons, and the finding that the absolute readout is possible and rests on a remainder that belongs to the correlations, not to the sample of neurons or stimuli.

\section{Scaling and population size}\label{app:scaling}
More capacity helps where labels are plentiful and not where they are scarce (Fig.~\ref{fig:a1}). Twin receptive fields go from 0.34 to 0.48 to 0.51 $\Rsq$ between 2.2M, 17M and 57M parameters, but the budgets differ: the 2.2M point is 12 epochs of 2,500 populations and was still improving at its last epoch, the 17M and 57M points are 40 epochs of 5,000. The same 2.2M model trained on 80,000 populations reaches 0.44, so most of the 2.2M-to-17M gap is schedule, not capacity. Orientation error is flat from 2.2M upward on both MICrONS substrates ($20.9^\circ$, $20.2^\circ$, $19.9^\circ$ on the twin at 2.2M, 17M and 57M) and moves by $1.5^\circ$ on Allen ($35.2^\circ$, $36.5^\circ$, $36.7^\circ$; one run per point on one mouse split, against a $3.2^\circ$ spread over the three mouse splits at 2.2M); only the 0.3M model is worse ($27.5^\circ$ on the twin and $27.8^\circ$ in vivo). Every model above 2M memorises its training neurons within 10 to 20 epochs and is early-stopped on the held-out selection slice. Population size beyond 256 to 512 neurons changes orientation error by at most $2^\circ$ in either direction, while twin receptive fields keep gaining slowly (Fig.~\ref{fig:a2}). Split-to-split variance on held-out mice exceeds seed variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:a4}).

\section{Controls}
\label{app:controls}
Controls on the MICrONS standard configuration (17M, 512-neuron populations, digital twin unless stated; Table~\ref{tab:controls}).

\paragraph{What the decoder reads.} With the three row statistics as the only input per neuron and no relational term, the error is $37.7^\circ$, raw and after alignment alike, so the scalars fix a weak frame on their own; with the Gram row alone and no statistics it is $20.3^\circ$ on the twin and $26.5^\circ$ in vivo, raw and aligned, so the relations carry the frame and the scalars are dispensable. (They are not a selectivity confound: their correlations with distance to the nearest cardinal axis are below 0.12 in magnitude and with gOSI below 0.16.) With the statistics plus the Gram entering only as an attention bias, which makes the decoder equivariant to the order of the neurons, the error is $19.9^\circ$ over three seeds, the same as the standard decoder ($20.2^\circ$), whose input layer also projects the raw Gram row in sample order.

\paragraph{Reference decoders that see the activity.} To measure what the relations discard, the same transformer was trained with each neuron's token built from its response vector instead of its Gram row, so that it sees the stimulus-aligned activity the Gram is computed from (Table~\ref{tab:ref}). Adding the Gram to it as an attention bias changes nothing. A linear readout of each neuron's own response vector, with no population context, does as well as the activity transformer on orientation ($20.2^\circ$ against $21.5^\circ$ in vivo, $15.8^\circ$ against $15.0^\circ$ on the twin), so on orientation the activity decoder is a per-neuron tuning readout whose only advantage over the relational decoder is the alignment of the responses to the stimulus. On receptive fields the transformer is ahead of the linear readout (0.37 against 0.34 in vivo, 0.80 against 0.68 on the twin), so there the population context adds something. Against the activity transformer, the relations retain about 80\% of the gain over the best constant on orientation and about 60\% of the $\Rsq$ on receptive fields (against the linear readout, about 80\% and about 70\%); the twin receptive-field gap is inflated by the twin's labels and responses coming from the same model. With test responses from movie frames the decoder never trained on, the activity decoder has no valid input, since its coordinates now index different frames, and it falls to the level of the best constant or below it ($49.5^\circ$ in vivo, $41.0^\circ$ on the twin, against $41.3^\circ$ for the best constant), while the relational decoder scores $33.8^\circ$ in vivo and $20.7^\circ$ on the twin. This shows which inputs each readout can be applied to, not which readout is better. The activity decoder transfers across animals under a shared stimulus protocol (Section~\ref{sec:allen}) but not across stimuli. The relations do both, because a correlation does not depend on which frames it was computed from, only on both neurons having seen the same ones. A decoder given the response vectors with the stimulus bins shuffled, the same shuffle for every neuron of a population, is blind to the stimulus but free to compute any relation among the neurons. It is no better than the Gram decoder on orientation and far below it on receptive fields (Table~\ref{tab:ref}), but it has to discover the relations under a fresh permutation for every population, so the comparison does not show whether richer statistics than the pairwise Gram exist.

\paragraph{Cross-stimulus test.} Training and selection Grams built from one random half of the stimulus bins and test Grams from the other half give $20.7^\circ$ on the twin (2,500 bins per half, raw responses rather than principal components; three seeds, 20.3 to 21.2) and $33.8^\circ$ in vivo (60 bins per half; 33.4 to 34.2), against $20.2^\circ$ and $25.4^\circ$ with shared bins. A control with the same 60-bin half on both sides gives $25.3^\circ$, so the in-vivo increase comes from the stimulus sample, not the smaller Gram: a correlation matrix over 60 movie bins partly reflects which frames it was built from, and one over 2,500 bins does not.

\paragraph{Scan and area.} The MICrONS neurons come from 13 scans in four areas, same-scan pairs correlate more strongly than cross-scan pairs, and the scans look at different parts of the screen, so a decoder that read scan identity from the Gram could predict each scan's mean receptive-field position and score a positive $\Rsq$ without knowing any neuron's position. The Allen decoder does exactly that (Section~\ref{sec:rf}), so we checked MICrONS the same way, centring predictions and labels per scan and per area before scoring. The receptive-field result holds: on a fourth seed of the Table~\ref{tab:microns} configuration, run with saved predictions, $\Rsq$ is 0.499 absolute and 0.498 within scan on the twin, 0.249 and 0.253 in vivo, and 0.47 (twin) and 0.22 (in vivo) within area. Only 2\% of the label variance lies between scans and 9\% between areas, so a scan-identity readout could reach at most 0.02. For orientation, a predictor that assigns every neuron its scan's or area's mean orientation scores $39^\circ$, and removing a separate rotation per scan or area changes the decoder's error by less than $0.2^\circ$. Decoders trained on V1 alone transfer to the other areas unevenly across seeds (Table~\ref{tab:controls}): to AL the aligned error is $32^\circ$ in all three seeds but only one seed lands on a rotated frame, and to RL one seed transfers and two do not. The RL row uses 512-neuron populations and the V1 and AL rows 128. With class-balanced training populations, V1 itself is decoded $9^\circ$ worse than with the standard training set, against $1.8^\circ$ for class balancing of the whole volume, because V1's cardinal-heavy test set rewards the prior that balancing removes.

\paragraph{Class balance and priors.} Preferred orientations are uneven in MICrONS (19\% of labelled neurons within $11^\circ$ of $0^\circ$, 25\% of $90^\circ$, 6 to 10\% in each oblique bin), so a decoder could gain from the distribution alone. It gains little: the best constant prediction has an error of $41.3^\circ$, a predictor that assigns every neuron the circular mean of its scan $39.0^\circ$ and of its area $38.7^\circ$, against $20.3^\circ$ for the decoder on the twin and $24.6^\circ$ in vivo (neuron split 2). Training and testing on populations drawn with equal numbers of neurons from each of the eight classes (64 per class) gives $22.0^\circ$ on the twin and $26.6^\circ$ in vivo (two seeds: 22.4, 21.5; 26.5, 26.7), raw and after global alignment alike, so the absolute frame does not depend on the class imbalance. The per-area errors are within $3^\circ$ of each other: the frame is read from a volume-wide sample, so an area whose label mode differs from the volume's peak is not mis-framed. On Allen the labels are nearly flat (14 to 20\% per orientation), the best constant is $43.9^\circ$, and the balanced error is $36.4^\circ$ against $35.7^\circ$ raw on the saved per-neuron predictions of held-out-animal split 0, which average 32 population draws per neuron (Table~\ref{tab:allen} reports the population-level error of the same run, $35.2^\circ$). The per-bin errors show, on both datasets, that the readout is cardinal: neurons preferring $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$ are decoded to $11^\circ$ to $12^\circ$ on the twin and to $25^\circ$ and $34^\circ$ on Allen, oblique ones to $37^\circ$ to $39^\circ$ on the twin and, on Allen, to $34^\circ$ and $35^\circ$ at $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ but $45^\circ$ at $120^\circ$ and $150^\circ$, and the predictions pile up on the two cardinal axes: 63 to 73\% of MICrONS predictions fall within $11.25^\circ$ of $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$ (two neuron splits per substrate) and 51 to 63\% of Allen predictions within $15^\circ$ (three mouse splits), the half-widths of the bins of Fig.~\ref{fig:bins}. The relations say how close a neuron is to a cardinal axis, the anisotropy of Section~\ref{sec:frame} seen from the decoder's side, and much less about which oblique it prefers.

\paragraph{A subtraction ablation that leaks labels.} To remove the class-level anisotropy from the input, we estimated the class-Gram residual (8 or 24 classes) on the labelled training half and subtracted the class-pair offset from every Gram entry, using each neuron's true class, at training and test. The twin error was unchanged ($20.7^\circ$ raw and aligned at 8 classes) and improved at 24 classes ($17.7^\circ$); the control that subtracts the whole class structure, which should destroy decoding, improved it further ($17.2^\circ$ raw, $15.6^\circ$ aligned). An offset keyed to the true class encodes that class in the Gram, through its jumps at class boundaries and its estimation noise, and the decoder reads it. No result of this ablation is used.

\paragraph{Synthetic populations.} 6,000 model neurons with von Mises tuning ($\kappa = 2$), preferred orientations either uniform or with half the neurons within about $12^\circ$ of a cardinal axis (count), tuning either isotropic or sharpened to $\kappa = 4$ at the cardinals (sharp), and a 120-frame stimulus that either covers orientation evenly or has half its frames near a cardinal axis (stim); responses with Gaussian noise; the 2.2M decoder on 512-neuron populations, epoch selected on the raw error as in every other run, five data and decoder seeds (Table~\ref{tab:syn}). With every switch off the class-Gram is circulant to within noise (neighbour-correlation range 0.01 against 0.04 to 0.14 with a switch on), and the decoder recovers the ordering but no frame. Sharper cardinal tuning and a cardinal excess of neurons fix the frame in at most one seed of five, alone, together, or at any strength ($\kappa$ up to 32; gain up to 5 times; two seeds each, all above $42^\circ$ raw); stimulus anisotropy fixes it in most seeds. The anisotropy the cortical data show (Section~\ref{sec:frame}) is modelled as a shared stimulus-driven component among like-tuned neurons: a random component per frame on 36 orientation nodes, smoothed over the circle with a von Mises kernel ($\kappa = 4$, half-width about $30^\circ$) so that only neurons with nearby preferences share it, enters each neuron's response with a weight peaked at $90^\circ$, with uniform counts, isotropic tuning and an even stimulus. Its class-Gram residual has the one-peaked form of the cortical one and correlates 0.39 to 0.48 with the in-vivo V1 residual; its size is set by the weight and reported as the residual's fraction of class-Gram variance (V1: 0.16 on the twin, 0.30 in vivo) together with the anisotropy factor (1.67, 1.99 and 1.92 at the three sizes). A global field over orientation with three Fourier modes was run at four strengths: it fixes the frame in two of five seeds at anisotropy factor 1.09 and residual fraction 0.00, four of five at 1.32 and 0.01, and five of five at 1.68 and 0.06 and at 1.75 and 0.07; its residual correlates negatively with the cortical one ($-0.38$), so the local field is the model reported. The spread across seeds is large in every cell where the frame is not fixed, because selection on the raw error then has no signal to select on, and the cell means are therefore reported with their range. The frame-fixed counts are unchanged with the raw-error threshold at $25^\circ$ or $35^\circ$, except that the two cells with a cardinal excess of neurons and no stimulus anisotropy, count and count + sharper tuning, drop to 0 of 5 at $25^\circ$ (their single frame-fixed seeds are at $27.1^\circ$ and $29.4^\circ$ raw). One run (all three switches, seed 0) never left its initial loss and recovered neither ordering nor frame; it is included in the mean.
